## 1. Purpose

This authority replaces the previous consolidated player-facing Writing-quality law with a stronger standard that preserves all of its useful character-voice, emotion, cadence, autonomy, Knowledge and semantic-safety requirements while closing the remaining narration-quality gap.

The problem being solved is not only weak dialogue.

A Story scene can fail even when:

- the facts are correct;
- the choices are meaningful;
- the characters technically have distinct voices;
- the Scene Board shows the right place;
- the runtime preserves the correct Chronicle state.

It still fails if the prose reads like a summary, a screenplay note, stage directions, a sequence of UI state changes, or a collection of clipped reaction lines rather than lived Story.

Canonical target:

> **The player should feel that a scene is unfolding around a person, not that a system is reporting the scene to them.**

And:

> **CE protects what happened. Writing makes the player feel it happening.**

# 4. The narration target — cinematic close prose, not admin prose

Unless a more specific Story authority deliberately requires another mode, Shinobi Chronicles should default to **cinematic, observer-bounded close-third-person narration** around the current focal character or legitimate scene observer.

That means narration may express:

- what the focal character notices;
- what catches their attention;
- what they physically feel;
- what they reasonably infer;
- what they remember if legitimately known;
- how time, space, sound and atmosphere register to them;
- internal thought when the scene benefits from it;
- emotional pressure without omnisciently explaining other minds.

Narration must not casually know another person's hidden motive, private thought or undisclosed identity merely because the author knows it.

The camera may be cinematic. The Knowledge boundary remains real.

### Strong target

Narration should feel like a **selective lens** rather than a security camera.

It does not catalogue the whole room.

It chooses what matters now.

What the prose notices should reveal:

- pressure;
- danger;
- attraction;
- distrust;
- exhaustion;
- curiosity;
- familiarity;
- grief;
- humour;
- anticipation;
- power imbalance;
- a character's way of seeing the world.

### Reject

Do not default to:

`Kakashi entered the room. Obito was there. Kakashi looked at him. Obito smiled. They walked outside.`

That is blocking, not Story.

# 5. Narration must have rhythm

Narrative prose has cadence just as dialogue does.

Sentence architecture should respond to the scene.

Use variation deliberately:

- short sentences for impact, shock, recognition, sudden danger or dry humour;
- longer flowing sentences for travel, reflection, atmosphere, mounting anticipation or emotional release;
- fragments when a focal character's attention fractures or locks onto something;
- paragraph breaks to control emphasis, timing and breath;
- repetition only when it creates pressure, humour, obsession, fear or emphasis.

Do not let the whole game collapse into one-line paragraph after one-line paragraph merely because a sparse Kakashi scene worked.

Do not let every scene become long lyrical paragraphs either.

Canonical rule:

> **The prose breathes at the speed of the moment.**

The approved Kakashi exemplar is a benchmark for specificity and character truth, **not a universal sentence meter**.

---

# 6. Focalisation should flavour narration without turning narration into dialogue

The narrator may remain grammatically third-person while the selected details, comparisons, emphasis and rhythm subtly reflect the focal character.

Examples of legitimate variation:

- Kakashi may register exits, hands, timing errors, distance and inconsistencies before decoration;
- Obito may notice social attention, challenge, embarrassment, praise, perceived unfairness or opportunities to prove himself;
- Hinata may register interpersonal tension, scrutiny, body posture and small signs of approval/disapproval more readily;
- Kushina may notice provocation, disrespect, protectiveness, social energy and emotional heat quickly.

This does **not** mean every paragraph must mimic the character's spoken slang.

It means POV selection can help the reader inhabit the Character rather than floating above them in generic omniscience.

---

# 7. Sensory detail must be selective and consequential

Use sensory detail when it changes how the moment feels or what the focal character perceives.

Potential channels include:

- sound;
- temperature;
- air movement;
- scent;
- texture;
- weight;
- distance;
- light;
- shadow;
- crowd density;
- pressure in the body;
- environmental motion.

Do not perform a five-senses checklist.

One exact sensory detail that belongs to the moment is stronger than six decorative ones.

Examples of useful sensory function:

- sandals scraping roof tile just before an ambush;
- rain making a weapon grip unreliable;
- food smoke making a quiet street feel domestic before violence intrudes;
- a room becoming noticeably quieter after a forbidden name is spoken;
- the weight of a sealed package becoming more noticeable after the player learns its stakes.

Sensory description should support Story, tension, character or memory — not prove that the writer can describe things.

---

# 8. Show and tell are both tools — use the right one

"Show, don't tell" is not absolute law.

Some information should be told quickly so the Story can move.

Use **showing** when the emotional experience, relationship, danger, humour, discovery or character behaviour is the point.

Use **telling** when compression is useful and the skipped material is not itself the scene's dramatic value.

### Expand when:

- the relationship changes;
- a character reveals themselves through behaviour;
- a choice consequence lands;
- danger becomes personal;
- a joke depends on timing;
- trust or suspicion shifts;
- an arrival matters;
- a goodbye matters;
- a Battle aftermath matters;
- the player should remember the moment.

### Compress when:

- routine travel contains no new pressure;
- repeated logistics add nothing;
- a mundane process has already been established;
- the only purpose is getting from one authored scene to another.

Canonical rule:

> **Expand the part the player is supposed to feel. Compress the part they only need to understand.**

---

# 9. Physical performance must express character, not decorate dialogue

Body language is not a mandatory gesture attached to every line.

Avoid mechanical patterns such as:

`line -> nod -> line -> sigh -> line -> look away`

Physical behaviour should do at least one useful thing:

- reveal emotion;
- contradict spoken words;
- reveal social posture;
- alter distance or threat;
- show confidence or uncertainty;
- show a habitual trait;
- create humour;
- carry an unspoken answer;
- change scene state;
- redirect attention.

Different people should embody the same emotion differently.

Embarrassment may produce:

- Obito rubbing the back of his head and talking too much;
- Kakashi becoming even more economical;
- Hinata shrinking physically or losing eye contact;
- Kushina becoming louder, faster or more defensive.

These are examples of differentiation, not universal deterministic reactions.

---

# 10. Dialogue should live inside action and thought

Dialogue is not a transcript separated from Story.

Strong dialogue scenes may weave together:

`attention -> line -> reaction -> movement -> silence -> answer -> thought/perception -> changed physical relation`

Action beats should belong to the speaker or observer and should clarify emotion, timing or physical state.

Use ordinary dialogue tags when clarity benefits. `Said` is not a failure.

Do not contort sentences to avoid simple attribution.

Do not overuse fancy speech verbs merely to sound literary.

Do not attach an adverb to every line when behaviour can carry delivery.

Written intonation should come from:

- word choice;
- punctuation used with restraint;
- sentence shape;
- interruption;
- repetition;
- silence;
- action beats;
- timing;
- relationship context.

---

# 11. Subtext is mandatory where people would not say everything directly

Characters should not constantly explain:

- what they feel;
- what they want;
- what their relationship means;
- what another person's action signifies;
- what the player should conclude.

Allow meaning to live in:

- avoidance;
- overreaction;
- sudden politeness;
- deliberately casual wording;
- a delayed answer;
- a changed subject;
- a held look;
- a refusal to look;
- a joke used defensively;
- saying less than expected;
- saying too much because someone is nervous.

Subtext must still respect Knowledge.

Mystery is not permission for arbitrary vagueness.

---

# 12. Internal thought — use it when it earns proximity

Internal thought is legitimate when POV supports it and when it gives the player something behaviour alone cannot efficiently provide.

Use it for:

- private contradiction;
- calculation;
- remembered context;
- unwanted emotion;
- self-deception;
- uncertainty;
- decision pressure;
- a conclusion the character does not say aloud.

Do not use internal monologue to dump CE state or solve mysteries for the player.

Do not repeat aloud what the narration has just stated internally.

Internal thought should deepen character proximity, not duplicate exposition.

---

# 13. Environmental narration should carry mood and movement through time

Transitions deserve prose when they change emotional or narrative temperature.

Examples:

- afternoon bleeding into dusk after a long day;
- market noise fading as characters enter an empty district;
- village warmth contrasting with an ANBU assignment;
- a familiar street feeling different after a death, betrayal or victory;
- two characters walking together when they normally separate.

Time passage should not always be represented by sterile labels such as:

`LATER`

`EVENING`

`THE NEXT DAY`

Those labels may be useful presentation markers, but prose should carry the change when the transition itself matters.

A transition can do Story work.

It can show:

- altered relationships;
- exhaustion;
- growing comfort;
- unresolved silence;
- dread;
- anticipation;
- a changed world.

---

# 14. Action and Battle-adjacent prose must remain spatially intelligible

Fast prose is not permission for spatial confusion.

In action-heavy scenes, the player should generally understand:

- who is where;
- what changed;
- what the immediate threat is;
- what object/custody state matters;
- why a new option is now available or unavailable.

Use concrete verbs and selective physical detail.

Do not narrate every punch, footstep or frame unless the sequence itself is the authored dramatic event.

Battle resolver truth remains authoritative.

Story prose may dramatize the committed Battle result but must not invent uncommitted injury, kill, custody, capability or tactical facts.

---

# 15. Emotional scenes need movement, not one static mood

A good scene usually has an emotional trajectory even when nothing explosive happens.

Examples:

- guarded -> curious -> amused;
- confident -> embarrassed -> defensive;
- tense -> relieved -> unsettled;
- affectionate -> frustrated -> vulnerable;
- triumphant -> reflective;
- suspicious -> convinced;
- playful -> suddenly serious.

The scene does not need a melodramatic turn.

But if every paragraph holds the exact same emotional temperature, the scene may feel dead even when the dialogue is technically good.

Writing should know:

- the emotional entry state;
- what changes it;
- the emotional exit state.

---

# 16. Figurative language and style

Metaphor, simile, imagery and lyrical phrasing are allowed.

Use them when they sharpen the moment.

Avoid:

- purple prose for its own sake;
- metaphors that the focal character would never plausibly frame the world through;
- repeated anime/light-novel clichés;
- generic phrases such as `time seemed to stop` or `an eternity passed` unless the specific scene earns them;
- overdescribing eyes, smirks, darkness, killing intent or chakra merely because the genre permits it.

Strong prose should feel specific to **this** place, **this** person and **this** Chronicle.

---

# 17. Avoid mechanical repetition and successor-chat tics

Successor chats commonly develop accidental house tics.

Audit for overuse of:

- `A beat.`;
- `For a moment...`;
- `Then...` at the beginning of many consecutive paragraphs;
- `His/Her eyes...` every few lines;
- `He/She nodded.`;
- `He/She sighed.`;
- `He/She looked away.`;
- repeated ellipses;
- single-line paragraphs for every sentence;
- constant dry understatement;
- constant sarcastic comeback;
- constant `flatly`, `quietly`, `softly`;
- generic smirking;
- generic clenched fists;
- stock blush/shy gestures regardless of character;
- every emotional change being announced by narration.

None of these devices are banned.

Repetition without intent is the problem.

The game must not inherit the current model/chat's favourite verbal habits.

---

# 18. Scene Board integration — the visual does not excuse weak prose

The Scene Board can show:

- place;
- actors;
- visible objects;
- speaker focus;
- approximate spatial state;
- arrival/departure;
- changed custody/threat/objective state.

Writing should therefore avoid redundant inventory description.

But visuals do not replace:

- temporal flow;
- emotional timing;
- character-specific reaction;
- internal attention;
- conversational rhythm;
- implication;
- atmosphere;
- relationship texture;
- connective Story movement.

Canonical rule:

> **The image tells the player where they are. The prose tells them what it feels like to be there now.**

---

# 19. Prose density should flex by scene purpose

There is no universal ideal paragraph length.

### High-pressure encounter

Prefer:

- clarity;
- sharp sensory selection;
- faster rhythm;
- short decision windows;
- spatial certainty.

### Intimate conversation

Allow:

- silence;
- thought;
- facial/body nuance;
- slower pacing;
- relationship-specific subtext;
- longer breathing room.

### Travel / transition

Prefer:

- selective atmosphere;
- meaningful time passage;
- relationship evolution;
- compression when nothing changes.

### Debrief / institutional scene

Avoid turning people into information terminals.

Use:

- role-specific behaviour;
- interruptions;
- reactions;
- questions;
- withheld information;
- hierarchy;
- physical setting;
- consequences from what was reported.

### Humorous scene

Protect timing.

Do not explain the joke after it lands.

# 20. Unified narration + performance acceptance gate

A substantial player-facing Story unit is not Writing-complete until it passes the prior consolidated acceptance tests **and** all narration-specific tests below.

### N1 — POV / focalisation test

Is the narration bounded to what the focal observer may perceive, think, remember or legitimately infer?

### N2 — selective-detail test

Are details chosen because they matter to character, mood, tension, action or memory rather than because the writer is inventorying the scene?

### N3 — rhythm test

Does sentence/paragraph rhythm change appropriately with the moment, or has the scene fallen into one repetitive cadence?

### N4 — compression test

Did Writing expand the parts worth feeling and compress the parts that only need understanding?

### N5 — physical-performance test

Do gestures and movement reveal something, or are they decorative stage directions attached to lines?

### N6 — subtext test

Are characters allowed to imply, avoid, conceal, overcompensate and misunderstand rather than explaining everything directly?

### N7 — internality test

Where internal thought appears, does it deepen character proximity without leaking hidden truth or duplicating exposition?

### N8 — transition test

When time/place/emotional temperature changes, does the scene feel that transition rather than merely label it?

### N9 — sensory-relevance test

Does sensory detail earn its place?

### N10 — action-clarity test

Can the player understand immediate spatial/causal state during action without needing a design document?

### N11 — emotional-trajectory test

Does the scene have an emotional entry state, meaningful movement and an exit state, even if subtle?

### N12 — prose-tic test

Has the current Writing chat/model accidentally repeated its favourite sentence patterns, gestures, adverbs or joke cadence throughout the scene?

### N13 — read-aloud test

Read the scene mentally as spoken/performed text. Does dialogue sound speakable? Does narration breathe naturally? Are pauses and emphasis earned?

### N14 — visual-redundancy test

Is the prose adding rhythm, perception, emotion, implication or change rather than merely redescribing what the Scene Board already shows?

### N15 — memory test

After reading, can the player remember a human moment, image, reaction or emotional turn — not merely the facts that were committed?

If these are RED, Writing remains RED even when the Story graph and dialogue facts are correct.

- character separation;
- natural rhythm;
- performed intelligence;

# 23. Successor Writing workspace boot law

A new Writing workspace must not begin substantial player-facing Story from its own default prose habits.

Before authoring, consume:

1. current Konoha Writing successor/deep-sweep authority;
2. this successor Story-performance / narrative-craft authority;
3. current character-specific voice/personality anchors;
4. current canon-first character authority where relevant;
5. exact current Story / branch authority;

Then perform a brief internal preflight:

- Who is the focal character?
- What do they notice first?
- What is the emotional entry state?
- What changes during the scene?
- What is each speaker's cadence here?
- What is being left unsaid?
- What should the player remember afterward?

A chat change must not produce an authorial personality reset.

## 2026-09-19 — Conversational Response and Personality-Profile Enforcement Addendum

**Stephen-directed status:** **BINDING EXTENSION TO THIS SINGLE ACTIVE WRITING-QUALITY LAW**

This addendum closes a recurring failure mode visible in institutional, debrief and hidden-evaluation scenes: a scene may contain technically correct questions and technically correct answers while still sounding like a form being completed.

Canonical rule:

> **People ask questions. People answer as themselves.**

A question is not merely a request for a fact. It is a social act received by a particular person in a particular emotional state, relationship and situation.

Therefore a substantial answer should normally be filtered through:

- the speaker's personality;
- their current emotion;
- their relationship to the questioner;
- what they know and do not know;
- what they want;
- what they are willing to reveal;
- how much they care;
- current stress, fear, anger, humour, sympathy, irritation, confidence or restraint;
- institutional role where relevant.

The factual payload may be identical while the human answer may be:

- thoughtful;
- humorous;
- understanding;
- sympathetic;
- apathetic;
- dry;
- unkind;
- fierce;
- warm;
- defensive;
- evasive;
- suspicious;
- irritated;
- embarrassed;
- frightened;
- amused;
- impatient;
- compassionate;
- contemptuous;
- professionally restrained;
- deliberately silent.

These are examples, not a closed taxonomy.

A character may:

- answer the literal question;
- answer the emotional meaning underneath it;
- challenge the premise;
- ask a question back;
- joke;
- deflect;
- refuse;
- over-explain;
- under-explain;
- change subject;
- answer with body language;
- remain silent.

### One-word answers

One-word or very short answers remain valid when they are **deliberate character performance**.

They are not the default merely because the factual answer is short.

Repeated structures such as:

`question -> one-word answer -> paraphrase -> one-word answer -> next question`

are a Writing RED flag unless the scene specifically earns that interrogation rhythm.

Canonical distinction:

> **Brief can be characterful. Repetitive factual ping-pong is not characterisation.**

A short answer should still carry attitude through word choice, timing, physicality, silence, relationship context or surrounding reaction when the moment matters.

### Debrief / institutional scene enforcement

Debriefs, command rooms, reports, police scenes, ANBU scenes and hidden evaluations must not degrade into information-terminal dialogue.

Questions should create human response.

Reports should create interpretation, disagreement, humour, discomfort, sympathy, suspicion, anger, restraint or other character-specific reaction where appropriate.

Characters present in the room should have their own perspective rather than existing only to repeat the report.

### Personality-profile requirement

The existing Writing-owned personality-baseline requirement is now mandatory preflight for recurring named characters.

Before substantial player-facing dialogue is authored for a recurring named character, Writing must:

1. consume the current durable personality / voice profile if one exists;
2. consume any more specific current canon/Chronicle modifier;
3. if no durable profile exists, create at least a minimum viable Writing-owned profile before treating the dialogue as complete;
4. preserve that profile across successor chats;
5. update the profile when Recorded History legitimately changes the character's presentation.

Character personality profiles guide expression. They do **not** manufacture World Truth, hidden Knowledge, biography, relationships or causal history.

Character Creation / Visuals owns visual identity and representation fidelity. It may consume these personality anchors for expressions/poses, but it does not own Story voice unless a later explicit cross-lane authority says otherwise.

The active profile registry begins at:

`Documentation/Story/Character_Personality_and_Voice_Profile_Registry_2026-09-19.md`

### Additional acceptance tests

A substantial dialogue scene is not Writing-complete until it passes:

- **Q1 — Human-answer test:** do answers sound like a person responding, not a database returning the requested field?
- **Q2 — Emotional-filter test:** does the answer reflect the speaker's actual current emotional/social posture where that posture matters?
- **Q3 — Personality test:** could a different character give the same line unchanged? If yes, strengthen voice unless interchangeability is deliberately intended.
- **Q4 — Question-shape test:** does the conversation vary naturally between questions, answers, reactions, interruptions, silence, counter-questions and voluntary statements?
- **Q5 — Ping-pong test:** has the scene fallen into repeated short interrogatory exchanges with no developing relationship, emotion or room dynamics?

Final lock:

> **Information is what the answer contains. Character is how the person gives it.**
>
> **A correct answer that erases personality is not finished Shinobi Chronicles dialogue.**
